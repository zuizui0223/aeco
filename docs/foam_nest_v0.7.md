# AECO v0.7 — foam as a fertilization environment and a prospective butterfly observation gate
2026-11-25. Published aggregate reanalysis and field design, not an empirical foam-to-butterfly link.

## New verified biological anchor
Muto & Kubota (2009), https://doi.org/10.1002/cm.20358, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19334064/ : Zhangixalus arboreus (as Rhacophorus arboreus) has external fertilization in viscous foam-nest medium and corkscrew-headed sperm adapted to locomotion in a viscous environment. Kyoto University summary https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/ja/archive/prev/news_data/h/h1/news6/2009/090409_1 . Foam is therefore part of the provider's fertilization environment, not solely embryo protection. This does not imply the foam increases fertilization probability relative to a defined alternative, nor that butterflies fertilize eggs on it. The cross-species hypothesis concerns a shared constructed space affecting butterfly mate encounters upstream of internal fertilization. No cross-species transfer of gametes is proposed.
Kusano et al. 1991, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00164291 ; Kasuya et al. 1996, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02514965 : multiple male participation in Japanese treefrog spawning. Do not infer genetic paternity from participation. Kasuya et al. 1987 https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/81520 is the historical cooperation test referenced by Byrne et al. 2026; independent full-text verification of its nest-size result is pending. The latter study reports no analogous relationship between participating male number and nest size in the Japanese species.

## Provider-side benchmark (African species, not a Japanese calibration)
Byrne et al. (2026), Evolution 80:1657–1671, https://doi.org/10.1093/evolut/qpag071, reports 134 Chiromantis xerampelina nests, with volume 47.12–1319.47 mL and F(1,132)=324.44 for the positive simple regression of volume on male number. Recomputed R2=0.7108. Volume range is ~28-fold; if geometrically similar, projected area would span ~9.22-fold. The latter is hypothetical geometry, not measured optical exposure. Do not infer attraction proportional to area; measure projected area, contrast, occlusion and access instead. The authors report 44 failed nests (40 desiccation, 4 blown down); 44/134 is 32.84%, not the printed 30.55%. Preserve discrepancy; no raw data available to resolve it. 33/40 desiccated nests were single-male nests, a composition of failures, not a group-specific failure risk. Paternity analysis supports multiple sires in this African system. No claim about Japanese foam volume, adult butterfly use, or causal cross-species effects.
Data DOI https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.9s4mw6mx8 . README documents five workbook sheets including 134 nests for morphology/success and 65 nests for churning synchrony. Exact download https://datadryad.org/downloads/file_stream/4716327 returned HTTP 403 in web access; container URL request failed DNS. No workbook received; no individual-level reanalysis. If obtained, audit the size/success denominators and analyze nest volume conditional on female size, report observational limits, and do not treat the African species as a Japanese proxy.

These published general ranges overlap but are not same-site observations, mating phenology, abundance estimates or occupancy probabilities. Prioritize forest-edge P. melete and retain P. rapae; similar-looking Pieris spp. may require unknown/group identification. Suzuki et al. (1977), https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/2050307417035476480, compares mating behavior of four Japanese Pieris: female avoidance behavior lasted longer in melete/canidia than napi/rapae in outdoor cages. Do not equate male approach with female receptivity or transfer the rapae UV mechanism to melete without testing. ## G0 field observation (next empirical step)
Target May–July 2027, local phenology checked first. Initial feasibility design (not a powered confirmatory trial): six independent ponds, aim for four natural nest–matched-control pairs per pond where naturally available; use only noninvasive observations and local authorization. Do not collect, move, touch or add substances to active nests. Select controls before butterfly outcomes: comparable branch height/angle/background, vegetation, illumination, water proximity, host/nectar availability, accessible wet surface, and equal observation volume and effort. Record nests by creation/discovery interval and observed stage (intact fresh, intact aged, collapse/residue, absent), not invented calendar ages. Include pre-formation matched footage where feasible and at least seven days after collapse when access permits; not a claim of residue persistence. Concurrent matched daylight video blocks and independent background transects; camera placement shall not shade or obstruct nests. Use a high-resolution wide view for approaches and close view for landing/proboscis/behavior; a motion-trigger-only detector is inadequate without a full-recording calibration. Do not disturb frogs or use night illumination. Use internal site codes and keep location records private.
Record minutes actually observed and calibration detection probability for approach/landing separately; classify passing, oriented approach, landing, proboscis contact, sustained feeding, courtship, completed mating separately. Proboscis contact not necessarily intake. Mark species and sex uncertainty. Use independent blinded manual review of candidate clips and a random sample of non-triggered footage; report sensitivity and false-positive rate with denominators. Count independent encounter events or uniquely identified individuals, not frame detections. Do not claim individual identity if no marking/reliable tracking. Analyze actual observed zeros, never treat missing camera footage as zero.
Outcome: difference in verified target species approach/landing/contact events per matched observation hour, with nest pair and pond/day dependence; event counts modeled with effort offsets and paired comparisons where sufficient. If six ponds are the only independent units, treat six as the effective units for the simple zero-detection calculation, not 24 pairs or hundreds of nest-hours. Initial result is feasibility. Natural comparisons remain subject to habitat selection and weather. A verified visit is an important positive-use event but cannot demonstrate mating or fertilization.

## Zero-detection sensitivity and next gates
Under independent standardized units, equal probability p of at least one visit and known conditional detection d, P(no detections)=(1-p*d)^n. One-sided 95% upper bound on p is min(1,(1-.05^(1/n))/d). 24 independent units with d=.8 give 14.67%; six give 49.13%. To detect p=.1 with probability >=.95, require 36 independent units at d=.8 or 59 at d=.5. These are prospective scenarios only, not species-specific parameter estimates or a power calculation for a treatment effect. Clustered sites, detection uncertainty and varying occupancy require hierarchical models and pilot estimates.
If adult pierids approach but never contact, prioritize visual landmark replica trials; if contact/feeding verified, prioritize measured foam cue chemistry trials; if there is no approach with adequate coverage, deprioritize mechanism locally, not proof of global absence. After evidence of compatible female/male encounters, proceed to v0.6 controlled visual x chemistry and ultimately v0.2 confirmed fertilized eggs per initially unmated female. Cross-species fertilization remains untested. Do not replace these gates with ordinary co-occurrence.
