# AECO v0.4 — Frog-mediated reproductive patches
2026-11-25. Focus: a frog-generated patch changes the spatial distribution of successful fertilization in Pieridae. This is not yet demonstrated. V0.3 is mathematical background, not the biological claim.

## Source audit and gaps
1. Beard, Vogt & Kulmatiski (2002), Oecologia, https://doi.org/10.1007/s00442-002-1071-9 : experimental coqui removal reduced leaf-wash NH4 by 71% and dissolved organic N by 60%. Terrestrial frog effects on chemistry are causal in that system. Not sodium, not a butterfly assay.
2. Corline et al. (2025), JAE, https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2656.14222 : wood frog tadpole development and hydrology generated aqueous NH4 and phosphate concentrations 56 and 14 times those in nearby wetlands without tadpoles. Does not establish increased sodium, terrestrial access, or butterfly visitation. Raw data DOI https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.bnzs7h4m1 : DOI identified in article; resolver returned 404 through this access route, no data acquired.
3. Beck et al. (1999), https://doi.org/10.1007/s004420050770 : Bornean Pieridae visited NaCl more frequently than protein resources, but also accepted albumin. Strong male bias overall, no mating or frog treatment.
4. Cannon (2018), https://rcannon992.com/2018/05/10/fighting-over-a-dead-frog/ : photographed red lacewing (Cethosia biblis, Nymphalidae) on a frog carcass in Thailand; natural history blog, not peer-reviewed and not a pierid, courtship or fertilization record.
5. Suetsugu & Sakakibara (2021), https://doi.org/10.1002/fee.2401 : Japanese tree frog attacked a Graphium sarpedon adult and damaged hindwing tips; failed capture. Direct negative interaction, not Pieridae. Stefanescu & Paramo (2010), https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/147476 : repeated Sahara frog predation on Vanessa cardui adults. Matsubara & Sugiura (2017), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aspen.2017.01.001 : frog consumed offered Pieris rapae larvae, not free-ranging adults. Keep distinct from adult mortality in the model.
6. Kusano, Sakai & Hatanaka (2006), https://www.thebhs.org/publications/the-herpetological-journal/volume-16-number-2-april-2006/434-07-ecological-functions-of-the-foam-nests-of-the-japanese-treefrog-i-rhacophorus-arboreus-i-amphibia-rhacophoridae : Japanese forest green tree frog (Zhangixalus arboreus, formerly Rhacophorus) foam nests provide thermal and nutritional functions for frog offspring; not a butterfly assay.
7. Fleming et al. (2009), https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2008.1939 : tungara frog foam contains proteins including surfactant and defense-related components. Protein content does not establish butterfly attraction; deterrence is plausible. Distinct species from Japanese frog.
8. Lamie et al. (2025), https://doi.org/10.1093/aesa/saaf007 : female attraction to puddling aggregations is unresolved, not demonstrated mating behavior.

## Mechanism priority
A. Reproductive construction: anurans create a foam nest during their own reproductive activity; after the offspring leave, residue/leachate might provide a localized, temporally persistent cue to butterflies. Primary Japanese candidate: Z. arboreus. This is a new candidate, not an established interaction. No pierid visitation to frog foam found in this targeted search. Foam may be inaccessible in shaded canopy or chemically deterrent. Observe noninvasively first; never remove active nests, eggs, tadpoles or wild frogs. Do not conflate protein concentration with accessible amino acids or sodium. Compare fresh and naturally spent nests only by nondestructive observation; any later sampling requires authorization.
B. Metabolic legacy: excretion and tadpole-conditioned water can alter chemistry; test accessible water-edge substrate after frog activity, not raw aquatic concentration. Frog abundance alone cannot identify a legacy effect.
C. Carrion: rare and potentially transient point sources, general vertebrate subsidy rather than frog-specific; opportunistic documentation only. No intentional killing, live bait, or wildlife translocation.
D. Negative mechanisms: active frog predation, avoidance, and male harassment may offset encounter facilitation. Frog presence and persistent material need not coincide.

## Time-lag and identifiability
A frog-created mating patch is distinguished from a preexisting wet habitat if (i) cue concentration and male arrival change after a frog-specific event, (ii) a comparable no-event patch does not change, (iii) the effect tracks naturally changing cue exposure, and (iv) direct observation and controlled contrasts confirm the mechanism. Event timing alone is not causal (rain, phenology, daylight, host plants). Use before/after repeated samples, matched empty patches, sham moist substrates and generic vertebrate or chemically matched controls. Select patches BEFORE butterfly outcomes are observed. Record substrate accessibility, Na, conductivity, ammonium, dissolved organic N, pH, moisture, light, temperature, floral/host resources, background butterfly abundance, frog activity/visibility, and observer effort. If sodium is unchanged, do not claim sodium mediation. If matched chemical control reproduces an effect, interpret as general chemistry not frog identity-specific recognition; a natural frog-generated patch can still act as a provider.

## Gated empirical sequence
G0: identify existing observations of butterfly contact with naturally created frog-associated substrates. For every record, independently review species, material, contact vs chance proximity, photo date, and activity. Grade direct adult Pieridae resource use separately from other butterfly families, larvae, and predation. No confirmed positive adult Pieridae record yet. Search scope targeted, not systematic exhaustive.
G1: field cameras and paired observers on naturally spent foam/residue and matched unoccupied or pre-event sites, matched day and weather; compare unique male and female arrivals and time spent. If no evidence of resource attraction at adequate precision, do not proceed with that mechanism. Validate that observed adults are puddling, not merely basking.
G2: randomized contained choice experiment, moisture and visual appearance controlled, permitted naturally abandoned material or safe chemical mimics. Cross cue chemistry with access to a site; do not transfer biological material between natural habitats. Use whole-array counts, not hotspot counts alone. No frog live exposure experiment required. Compare equally distributed versus concentrated cues of matched total quantity to identify spatial effect, and standardized pairing to identify physiology.
G3: track initially unmated females, compatible encounters, completed mating and sperm storage, confirmed fertilized eggs and hatch separately. Primary fraction with >=1 confirmed fertilized egg within fixed horizon (v0.2); postmating mortality, delays, egg laying and missing follow-up explicit. A mating image cannot identify fertilization or mating initiation. Estimate landscape-wide change, not only displacement.

## Direct frog-related model extension
Separate a hotspot formed by a persistent frog-derived cue from risk of contemporaneous frog presence. Fixed female locations, fixed male fractions, K=10, male hotspot share=.6, uniform completed mating hazard h=.5 over unit horizon; no remating. Assume a constant hotspot-only lethal/irreversible pre-first-mating loss hazard d (not measured) and no extra loss outside. The simplified probability of success in hotspot is q*a/(a+d)*(1-exp(-(a+d))) with a=Kmh; outside q*(1-exp(-b)), b=K(1-m)h/(K-1). In this toy model success is immediate upon successful first mating (no later mortality/delay); not an actual egg-level model. d=0 represents a hypothetical risk-free legacy, NOT observed absence of risk. d>0 represents extra competing loss, NOT an estimated frog predation rate. This models extra loss of opportunity, not larval predation. At fixed male attraction and female use, higher loss weakens the facilitation and can make it impossible for any female occupancy to beat uniform. No numerical coefficient links published NH4 or PO4 concentration to butterfly attraction or d. Script and results in repository.
